FIELD NOTES · 17 NOVEMBER 2026

New connections, encounters around bogs, Irish literature, creative writing, and the beginnings of a sense of place.

Another six weeks have gone by so quickly…

I don’t need to look out the window to know how windy and rainy it is. The soundscape in my living room is already conjuring up the perfect image of the weather around me, and I find comfort in the warmth of these walls as I listen out for the next howl, the next drop, the next silence. It’s 17 November and, with talk of snow coming to the UK, I am reminded of the adage we have in Emilian according to which the first day of snow predicts how many more snowfalls you’ll have over the winter. Seventeen snowfalls sounds amazing to me… I can only hope!

October has been a very busy and exciting month to have lived through. Thanks to the generous support of Dónall at Meitheal Pleanála Teanga an Iarthuaiscirt, I became aware of some incredible projects being run in Ireland on bogs: Project Errigal and MOSAIC.

I am looking forward to connecting with them and seeing where our projects overlap and how we can help each other. Likewise, as I was walking around campus the other week, I caught sight of WaterLANDS on one of those digital screens showcasing research at the university. WaterLANDS is a European project on wetlands, co-led by Shane McGuinness at UCD, which has just wrapped up with a celebratory event in Venice last week. I am still feeling disappointed in myself for not being able to attend because I was unwell, but I hope I can connect with the artists and colleagues who worked on the project to pick their brains about working in schools (luckily, they have so many resources that I can draw on) and creating card games.

This feels to me like one of those moments when things are happening around bogs and research on restoration is opening up to the role of language and relationality. It’s probably no surprise, nor coincidence, that my project was funded, as it fits right in. On 12 October, I attended the annual Irish Studies gathering at the Museum of Literature Ireland, where Dr Ailbhe Ní Ghearbhuigh presented on bogs in Irish literature: “Lig dom an bhroinn ama fúm a fheá: an portach i nualitríocht na Gaeilge”. I enjoyed listening to Irish being spoken for an entire hour. I know the lecture was about bogs, and I picked up the odd word here and there, but even though I didn’t understand what was being said, I enjoyed being in the moment: a moment I can only wish for Emilian, a moment when people who speak the minority language come together to talk about intellectual and mundane things alike.

The following evening, I attended a lecture at Tailors Hall on the Nature Restoration Regulation: Last Best Hope for Peatland Rewilding? Beautiful pub, by the way!

For once, it would seem, I came up with the right idea at the right time. All these activities happening around bogs are galvanising and feel like a real support network: people I can reach out to who have experience with these landscapes that I am slowly acquiring in my own way. I am creating a sense of place for myself and the bogs.

Sense of place is a concept that I was able to explore both intimately and collaboratively in October, when I attended a Mersey Forest workshop in St Helens. The workshop brought together a range of projects and researchers working both within and beyond academia, whether in local authorities, Forestry England or universities. In the afternoon, we signed up for different sessions, and I attended a creative writing workshop with Maria Gulina titled “Trees as Cultural Heritage (and the Stories They Tell)” and a hands-on workshop, “Co-designing Treescapes with Children and Young People”, as part of the ‘Voices of the Future’: Collaborating with Children and Young People to Re-imagine Treescapes project.

I did not leave empty-handed. A toolkit on working with schools that I will undoubtedly be drawing on in my project, and a poem about my childhood pine tree, are the little treasures I am stowing away. I am going to share the very rough draft of the poem here, with the intention of tweaking it and using it in my monograph. It’s unnerving, as I’ve never shared my creative writing before, but being vulnerable is part of the fun called life.

To top it all off, on 2 November I was in Bologna to give a guest seminar to MA students, having been invited by Annaclaudia Martini and colleagues at the Memory Lab. What an experience to be back in Bologna, on the other side of the ‘cattedra’, sharing my research interests with such an engaged group of students.

As I weave my way through the meanderings of an ethics application, I already find myself reminiscing about these special memories and romanticising the future, thinking about the work I will be doing with students and communities, and the surprises that the archival work might conceal. I look forward to my fieldwork and am already planning, in my head, the illustrated book that will hopefully come out of this project. I am the only person working on this project, yet I have already met so many people, and the joy of sharing these moments with others is too precious not to try to involve them in it.

The right tree

Born. Born only to be displaced, transported, re-emplaced.

Growing strong and tall, increasingly surrounded by vehicles whizzing by, cyclists riding and gazing, fewer birds chirping about, but louder, angrier, lonelier cries coming from inside that door.

I grew up behind that door – separated by bricks and wood, part me and part you.

An evergreen for an ever-bleak childhood. A constant presence in an intermittent feeding of care, where absence was the only certainty.

Towering over our suffering while looking strong and grandiose for everyone else to see. It is no wonder that she chose you. Stoic in her persistence of loving despite all, you – only you – were always the right choice.
